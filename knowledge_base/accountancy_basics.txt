Accountancy Fundamentals (Class 11)

Book keeping is the process of recording, in a systematic manner, the monetary
transactions of a business in the books of account maintained for the purpose.
Accounting goes a step further: it involves recording, classifying and
summarising transactions, and then interpreting the results so that owners,
managers, lenders and regulators can make informed decisions.

The accounting cycle followed by most businesses is: identify a transaction,
record it as a journal entry, post the entry to the relevant ledger accounts,
balance the ledger accounts, prepare a trial balance, and finally prepare the
financial statements (trading account, profit and loss account, and balance
sheet).

A journal entry records a transaction using the double-entry principle: every
transaction affects at least two accounts, one of which is debited and the
other credited, such that total debits always equal total credits for that
entry. For example, when a business buys furniture for cash, the Furniture
account is debited (an asset increases) and the Cash account is credited (an
asset decreases).

A ledger is a set of accounts to which journal entries are posted. Each ledger
account has a debit side and a credit side; the difference between the two
sides, at any point, is called the balance of that account. Asset and expense
accounts normally carry debit balances; liability, capital and revenue
accounts normally carry credit balances.

A trial balance is a statement, prepared on a particular date, listing the
debit and credit balances of all ledger accounts. If the double-entry
principle has been followed correctly, the total of the debit column should
equal the total of the credit column. A matching trial balance does not
guarantee there are no errors (for example, an entry omitted entirely, or
posted to the wrong account of the correct type, will not be caught by the
trial balance), but a mismatch definitely indicates an error somewhere.

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed asset (such
as machinery or a vehicle) over its useful life, reflecting wear and tear or
obsolescence. The two most commonly taught methods are the Straight Line
Method, where an equal amount of depreciation is charged every year, and the
Written Down Value (Reducing Balance) Method, where depreciation is charged
as a fixed percentage of the asset's remaining book value each year, so the
depreciation amount reduces over time.

A bill of exchange is a written, unconditional order signed by the maker (the
drawer), directing a certain person (the drawee) to pay a certain sum of
money, on demand or at a fixed or determinable future date, to a specified
person or to the bearer. It is commonly used in credit transactions between a
seller and a buyer to formalise the buyer's promise to pay on a future date.
