Financial Statements

Financial statements are the formal, structured reports prepared at the end of
an accounting period (usually a year) that summarise a business's financial
performance and financial position. For a trading/manufacturing business the
core statements taught at the school level are the Trading Account, the
Profit and Loss Account, and the Balance Sheet.

The Trading Account shows whether the buying and selling of goods, during the
period, resulted in a gross profit or a gross loss. It compares net sales
against the cost of goods sold (opening stock plus net purchases plus direct
expenses, minus closing stock). The resulting figure, gross profit or gross
loss, is carried forward to the Profit and Loss Account.

The Profit and Loss Account starts from the gross profit (or loss) and
further deducts indirect/operating expenses (such as salaries, rent,
depreciation, interest paid) and adds indirect incomes (such as interest
received, commission received), arriving at the net profit or net loss for
the period. Net profit increases the owner's capital; a net loss reduces it.

The Balance Sheet is a statement, prepared as on a particular date (not for a
period), showing the assets, liabilities and capital of the business. It is
based on the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital. Assets are
listed either in order of liquidity or in order of permanence, depending on
the format followed; common asset categories are fixed assets (land,
building, machinery, furniture), and current assets (cash, bank, debtors,
closing stock). Liabilities are split into long-term liabilities (such as
long-term loans) and current liabilities (such as creditors, bills payable,
outstanding expenses).

Financial Statement Analysis (a Class 12 topic) uses techniques such as
comparative statements, common-size statements, ratio analysis, and cash flow
statements to interpret the financial statements more deeply -- for example,
to judge liquidity, solvency, profitability and efficiency.

Ratio Analysis expresses a relationship between two accounting figures.
Commonly taught ratios include: the Current Ratio (current assets divided by
current liabilities, a measure of short-term liquidity, with 2:1 traditionally
considered a satisfactory benchmark); the Quick Ratio (liquid assets, i.e.
current assets excluding stock and prepaid expenses, divided by current
liabilities, a stricter liquidity measure, with 1:1 often taken as a
satisfactory benchmark); the Debt-Equity Ratio (a solvency measure comparing
long-term debt to shareholders' funds); and the Gross Profit Ratio and Net
Profit Ratio (profitability measures expressing gross/net profit as a
percentage of net sales).

A Cash Flow Statement classifies cash inflows and outflows during a period
into three activities: Operating Activities (cash effects of the core revenue-
generating operations), Investing Activities (cash effects of buying/selling
fixed assets and investments), and Financing Activities (cash effects of
changes in the size and composition of owners' capital and borrowings, such
as issuing shares or repaying a loan). It helps users understand a company's
ability to generate cash, which the Profit and Loss Account alone does not
directly show.
